Moonlight

May 2026

About Writing Cities Reviews Recommendations Snail Mail

Moonlight doesn't tell you what to feel. It just puts you inside Chiron's world and trusts you to figure it out.

The film is structured in three acts, each named after what other people call him: Little, Chiron, Black. That's already the whole thesis. A boy who has spent his entire life being seen through other people's eyes, trying to find something in himself that belongs only to him.

What Barry Jenkins does with color is extraordinary. In the scenes between Chiron and his mother Paula, the frame explodes into neon pinks and greens, colors that feel almost hallucinatory, the way a child experiences a parent's rage when it makes no sense and can't be predicted. Chiron's side of those same scenes sits in muted blues and yellows, undersaturated, sad. Two people in the same room living in completely different emotional realities. You don't need the dialogue. The color tells you everything.

And then there's Juan's house. Soft blues, light greens, white. The costumes, the walls, the light itself. The moment Chiron walks in, you feel the temperature change. This is what safety looks like when a child has never been taught to recognize it.

The camera moves like it's alive. When Juan is introduced, it circles him slowly and steadily. He's calm, he owns his space, and the camera reflects that. Minutes later, when Chiron is being chased by bullies, it becomes handheld, shaky, almost panicked. Jenkins doesn't tell you these two characters live in different worlds. He shows you by changing the grammar of the film itself.

The swimming scene is the one that stays with me the most. Juan teaching Chiron how to float in the ocean, the underwater shots, the cool blue light, the way the camera follows the boy's body through the water so gently, like it doesn't want to startle him. It feels like the only moment in the film where Chiron's body is held without consequence. Jenkins films it with such tenderness that it becomes almost a baptism, not into religion, but into the possibility that someone could care about him.

The film is about identity and sexuality, yes, but it's also about what happens to a person when they grow up without a mirror that reflects them honestly. The bullying, the absent father, the mother lost to addiction. By the time we reach Black in the third act, we're watching someone who has spent years building armor so convincing he's almost disappeared inside it.

The reunion with Kevin at the diner is almost unbearably quiet. The camera stays close to both of them, soft focus on everything else. All that history sitting in a single booth, neither of them quite able to say what they mean. Jenkins doesn't rush it. He lets it breathe, lets us sit inside the discomfort the same way Chiron has been sitting inside his whole life.

What the film argues is that softness is not weakness. That being truly seen is one of the rarest things that can happen to a person. It earns every emotion it asks of you.

I've thought about it more than almost anything else I've watched.
