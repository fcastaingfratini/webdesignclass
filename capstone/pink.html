On Reclaiming the Color They Told Us to Put Away

I love the color pink. Since I was a young girl, the more glitter, textured, sequins, and fun patterns (and pink), the more I was going for it. As many of us do, there comes a moment when we start gravitating away from pink. I’d say 12 or 13 is probably a good estimation. Beige became the new pink, neutral rooms, white bedding, cream colored clothing. We had to act mature and cool; we had to prove to the world that we should be taken seriously. And no, we weren’t women yet - but that was our survival strategy. Dress like a smart girl, dress in black and neutrals, and stray away from anything that calls too much attention to yourself. This way, they’ll at least maybe listen to us - maybe they will be able to focus on our words, our brains, our thoughts, all of which were too complex for them to understand, by the way. And little by little, each stuffed animal lost its place on our bed. Each funky top went to the donation pile, and each childhood keepsake, like the fluffy pens and fruit-shaped silicone phone cases, was thrown away or stored under our beds to collect dust.

This is not a new story. Women have been erasing themselves for centuries in order to be taken seriously, borrowing men’s names, flattening their voices, shrinking their presence. George Eliot was Mary Ann Evans first. The Brontës published under the names Currer, Ellis, and Acton Bell. Pseudonyms weren’t just pen names; they were survival strategies, the literary equivalent of putting away the glitter and reaching for the beige. And even then, even disguised, their work was dismissed, debated, gatekept from the walls of museums and the pages of canons written by and for them.

What often survived were the journals. Women’s diaries and private writings have always been the most honest record we have, unperformed, unfiltered, written for no audience but the self. And yet they are precisely what gets left out of official history. The journals give us the context back. They are the corrective text. They are what’s left when everything else has been edited out.

But now, years later, I love anything pink again, though it didn’t happen the way you might expect. I didn’t wake up one morning and find my way back to it. I made a commitment to myself, even while I still hated it. I forced myself to be a powerful woman in the world while wearing pink. I chose it deliberately, stubbornly, before the feeling caught up with the act. And slowly, it did. Pink became resignified, no longer the color I had been shamed out of, but the color I was reclaiming. I put on extra amounts of blush and lip gloss each morning. I choose the pink token every time I play a board game with my friends.

Pink is a secret code. The ones who get it, get it. The ones who don’t, don’t. And for those who mock us for wearing it, we see you. We’ve always seen you. What you call frivolous, we call armor. What you dismiss, we’ve learned to weaponize. Pink is my political force, a language for when words aren’t available, when the world pushes them back inside, into the journal, into the diary, into the private record that history keeps trying to lose. We’ve been here before. We know how to hide things in plain sight. We know how to make the dismissed thing into the most important thing in the room.

Pink feels like a rebellion. Pink feels like fighting for what’s been taken away from us all those years ago. And if you don’t understand why — that’s exactly the point.
