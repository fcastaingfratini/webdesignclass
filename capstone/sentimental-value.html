Sentimental Value

April 2026

About Writing Cities Reviews Recommendations Snail Mail

From the very first frame, Sentimental Value does something quietly radical: it treats a house as a character.

Not just any house — the house where Nora grew up, where her sister grew up, where their mother just died, and where their absent father has now returned after fifteen years of silence. A house that also belonged to his parents, and to their parents before them. You begin to understand, slowly, that this building holds more history than any single person can carry.

The film is Joachim Trier's latest, and it moves with his signature patience — unhurried, layered, precise. There are moments that feel slow, but the dialogue and cinematography are so carefully constructed that you don't mind. You lean in instead of pulling away.

The story picks up when Gustave, a celebrated filmmaker who hasn't made anything in over a decade, learns of his ex-wife's death and reappears in his daughters' lives. He wants to make a film about his mother's story — and he wants Nora to star in it. The problem is that Nora is already an actress, a theater actress, and her father has never quite respected that. She has no husband, no children, and a life that seems to exist permanently in the shadow of his legacy.

What makes the film so rich is how it layers its themes without ever announcing them. It's about art, yes, and about family. But more than anything, it's about how trauma moves through generations — how the unfinished stories of one life quietly filter into the next.

For Gustave, the past is raw material. For Nora, it's just her life.

That tension holds the entire film together. And the house holds all of it — walls that seem to remember versions of this family that no longer exist, silences passed down like inheritance.

Sentimental Value doesn't offer resolution so much as recognition. What do we do with the stories we've inherited — do we repeat them, transform them, or finally find a way to let them be? It stays with you long after it ends. Which is, I think, exactly what it intended.
